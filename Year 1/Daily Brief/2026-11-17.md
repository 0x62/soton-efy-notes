## 09:00–10:00 · Digital Systems · 32/1015

**Prof Mark Zwolinski. Scheduled topic: Microprocessors 1.** The current *Microprocessor 1* slides introduce a small processor built from the logic blocks studied so far. The exact transition from yesterday's sequential-design material is unconfirmed; no fresh lecture transcript was available for this briefing.

- The **program counter (PC)** selects an instruction from instruction memory. The **instruction register (IR)** holds that instruction, and a decoder interprets its operation code.
- The **arithmetic and logic unit (ALU)** performs operations on data. A working register retains results, while data memory supplies operands and stores outputs.
- An instruction describes both an **operation** and an **operand**. In this teaching processor, an eight-bit instruction has a three-bit opcode and a five-bit operand. These are the course's illustrative encodings, not AVR or ARM machine code.
- **Direct addressing** treats the operand as a data-memory address. In `ADD c`, the processor reads the value stored at address `c`; it does not add the address itself.
- **Immediate addressing** uses the operand as a literal value. The separate `ADDI` opcode tells the datapath multiplexer to supply that literal to the ALU instead of data read from memory. Parameterised SystemVerilog modules keep instruction width and opcode width explicit.

**Worked example.** The slides translate `a = b + 2` into `LD b`, `ADDI 2`, `ST a`. If `b` is at address 1 and `a` at address 3, the encodings are `000 00001`, `101 00010`, and `001 00011`. With 7 stored at `b`, the working register receives 7, the ALU adds the literal 2, and 9 is stored at `a`. Replacing `ADDI 2` with `ADD 2` would instead add whatever is stored at address 2.

**Takeaway:** follow the data selected by each instruction, not just its binary pattern. The opcode determines what the operand means.

**Useful prep:** label PC, IR, decoder, ALU, working register and the two memories on a simple block diagram; trace the three instructions above through it.

## 14:00–17:00 · T12: RL Filters and RLC Circuits · 59/2213

**ELEC1300 laboratory teaching team; no individual demonstrator is specified.** This is one afternoon session, not a double lab. The work links a physically wound inductor to measured filter and resonance behaviour.

- Wind the specified coil and compare its estimated inductance with **LCR-bridge measurements of inductance, resistance and quality factor**. An open ferrite rod is not an ideal closed magnetic circuit, so a simple permeability calculation is only an estimate.
- For the **RL high-pass filter**, take the output across the inductor. In the ideal model, $H(j\omega)=j\omega L/(R+j\omega L)$: low frequencies are attenuated and high frequencies approach unity gain.
- The ideal cutoff is $f_c=R/(2\pi L)$. At that point the amplitude is $1/\sqrt2$ of the passband value, approximately −3 dB. Coil resistance and generator impedance can change the practical response.
- The lab specifies a **15 Ω resistor**, a Bode plot with **at least 20 points**, and a suggested RL sweep of **40 Hz–800 kHz at 5 V peak-to-peak**. Compare the changes when two coils are connected in series or parallel; coupling and losses can prevent ideal doubling or halving.
- The driven RLC experiment adds **10 nF** and uses a **5 V peak-to-peak sine sweep up to 400 kHz**. Predict resonance using measured inductance: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. Measure node voltages with a common reference and subtract channels to obtain the voltage across the inductor, as directed by the lab notes. Do not put grounded probe clips on different circuit nodes.

**Worked example, illustrative only.** If an LCR bridge measured $L=1.0\,\mathrm{mH}$, a lossless-coil model with $R=15\,\Omega$ would give $f_c=15/(2\pi\times0.001)\approx2.39\,\mathrm{kHz}$. Adding $C=10\,\mathrm{nF}$ would give $f_0\approx50.3\,\mathrm{kHz}$. These are example calculations, not predicted results for the supplied rod.

**Takeaway:** use the measured component values to explain the response, and keep ideal predictions distinct from the behaviour of the real coil.

**Useful prep:** distinguish amplitude, power and decibels; identify the voltage reference for each measurement. The optional cascaded band-pass extension follows the main measurements. The full numbered preparation remains separate from this briefing.

## 17:00–18:00 · Mathematics · 46/3001

**Bing Chu. Week 9: second-order linear ODEs.** The dated announcement assigns this week to the theory and engineering applications of second-order equations. The precise division of those topics between individual lectures is not specified; the following covers the published week-wide material.

A second-order equation contains a second derivative. Its solution normally needs two independent constants, fixed by two initial conditions such as position and velocity. For a homogeneous constant-coefficient equation,

$$y''+ay'+by=0,$$

trying $y=e^{rt}$ gives the characteristic equation $r^2+ar+b=0$. Its roots determine the form of the solution:

- Distinct real roots: $y=C_1e^{r_1t}+C_2e^{r_2t}$.
- Repeated root $r$: $y=(C_1+C_2t)e^{rt}$; repeating the same exponential alone does not provide two independent solutions.
- Complex roots $\alpha\pm j\beta$: $y=e^{\alpha t}(C_1\cos\beta t+C_2\sin\beta t)$.

The engineering examples use $my''+cy'+ky=0$ for a damped mass–spring system. Mass, damping and stiffness determine the characteristic roots, which distinguish decaying non-oscillatory motion from damped oscillation. For a driven equation, the full solution combines the homogeneous response with a particular solution for the forcing.

**Worked example.** Solve $y''+3y'+2y=0$, with $y(0)=1$ and $y'(0)=0$.

1. Factor the characteristic equation: $(r+1)(r+2)=0$, giving roots −1 and −2.
2. Write $y=C_1e^{-t}+C_2e^{-2t}$.
3. The first initial condition gives $C_1+C_2=1$.
4. Differentiate: $y'=-C_1e^{-t}-2C_2e^{-2t}$. The second condition gives $-C_1-2C_2=0$.
5. Solving gives $C_1=2$, $C_2=-1$, hence $y=2e^{-t}-e^{-2t}$. Substitution checks both the equation and initial conditions.

**Takeaway:** the characteristic roots choose the solution form; the initial conditions choose the constants.

**Useful prep:** practise factoring quadratics and differentiating exponentials, then work through the two initial-condition equations. The associated week-9 tutorial sheet supports both the theory and engineering applications.
