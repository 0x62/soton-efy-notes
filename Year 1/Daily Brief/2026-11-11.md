### Three interfaces to distinguish

- **UART sends framed data without a shared clock wire.** Both ends must agree on baud rate and frame format. Connect one device's transmit line to the other's receive line, and provide a common ground. A logic-level UART is not directly interchangeable with the higher-voltage RS-232 electrical interface.
- **SPI sends data using a clock and a device-select signal.** The controller supplies the clock; MOSI and MISO carry opposite directions of data. Clock polarity and phase determine which edge changes data and which edge samples it. A mismatch can produce plausible-looking but incorrect bytes.
- **I²C uses shared clock and data lines, with addressed devices and acknowledgement bits.** The lines need pull-ups. Devices pull a line low or release it rather than actively driving it high, allowing several devices to share the bus.

These are different ways to solve the same problem: agreeing which bit is being transferred and when it should be read. A register setting only makes sense in the context of the complete connection and timing.

### A small timing check

An illustrative **9600-baud, 8-N-1 UART** frame contains one start bit, eight data bits, no parity bit and one stop bit: **10 transmitted bits per byte**.

$$t_{\text{frame}}=\frac{10}{9600}\approx1.04\text{ ms}.$$

That gives a maximum continuous payload rate of **960 bytes per second**, before any gaps or application overhead. Baud rate and useful byte rate are not the same quantity.

**Useful clinic questions:** if a program transmits nothing, check whether the problem is wiring, pin configuration, baud configuration or waiting on the wrong status flag. If data is corrupted, compare the frame settings and clock assumptions at both ends. Bring the smallest failing example and describe the expected and observed behaviour.

### What the graph means

For a **series RLC circuit** driven by a sinusoidal voltage,

$$Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right),\qquad
|Y|=\frac{1}{|Z|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}.$$

Admittance tells you the current response per volt: $|I|=|V_s||Y|$.

- **Below resonance**, $1/(\omega C)>\omega L$. Capacitive reactance dominates, so current leads the source voltage.
- **At resonance**, the two reactances cancel in the total impedance. The condition is $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, giving $Z=R$, maximum admittance $1/R$ and current in phase with the source.
- **Above resonance**, $\omega L>1/(\omega C)$. Inductive reactance dominates, so current lags the source voltage.
- At very low frequency, the capacitor limits current. At very high frequency, the inductor limits current. The current therefore has a peak between those extremes.

The **impedance graph has a minimum** at resonance, while the **admittance/current graph has a maximum**. Increasing resistance lowers the admittance peak. These statements describe a frequency sweep in sinusoidal steady state; they are different from the growing voltage-versus-time response of an ideal lossless LC circuit driven exactly at resonance.

### Worked illustration: cancellation does not mean zero component voltage

Take $R=10\,\Omega$, $L=0.10\,\mathrm H$, $C=10\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and a **2 V RMS** source. These are illustrative values, not a problem-sheet answer.

1. $LC=0.10\times10\times10^{-6}=10^{-6}$, so $\omega_0=1000\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and $f_0=1000/(2\pi)\approx159.2\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
2. At resonance, $X_L=\omega_0L=100\,\Omega$ and $X_C=1/(\omega_0C)=100\,\Omega$.
3. The total impedance is just $10\,\Omega$, so $I=2/10=0.20\,\mathrm A$ RMS.
4. Each reactive component has a voltage magnitude $0.20\times100=20\,\mathrm V$ RMS. Their phasors point in opposite directions, so they cancel in the loop sum while the resistor has the source's 2 V across it.

**Useful prep:** sketch $X_L$, $X_C$, $|Z|$ and $|Y|$ against frequency. Mark the same resonant frequency on all four, then explain the phase of the current on each side without relying on memorised signs.

### The problem you will discuss

Two blocks slide without friction on a level surface, with a wall on their left. Collisions are perfectly elastic. The task connects mechanics to a C simulation:

1. Derive the two post-collision velocities from conservation of momentum and kinetic energy.
2. Write a C program that counts both block–block collisions and collisions of the left block with the wall.
3. Check the equal-mass case, then increase the mass of the second block through $100$, $10\,000$, $1\,000\,000$, and so on, and investigate the pattern in the collision count.

For masses $m_1,m_2$ and pre-collision velocities $u_1,u_2$, the sheet's target equations are

$$v_1=\frac{(m_1-m_2)u_1+2m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2},\qquad
v_2=\frac{2m_1u_1+(m_2-m_1)u_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$

### What to understand before coding

- **Velocity has a sign.** With rightward positive, the sheet's initial second-block velocity is **$u_2=-1$**, not $+1$. The first block starts stationary. A wall collision reverses the first block's velocity.
- **Both new velocities use the old values.** Calculate $v_1$ and $v_2$ into temporary variables before updating the simulation state. Otherwise the second equation may accidentally use the already-updated first velocity.
- **Count events, not loop iterations.** A small time step is not itself a collision. Your program must distinguish a wall hit from a block–block impact and avoid counting one contact repeatedly.
- **Use physical checks as tests.** A block–block collision preserves total momentum and kinetic energy. The wall can change the blocks' total momentum, so do not apply that momentum test across a wall collision; the block's speed is preserved there.
- **Stop when no further collision is possible.** For the usual left-block/right-block ordering, both moving away from the wall is not sufficient if the left block is still catching the right one.

**First test:** the sheet supplies $m_1=m_2=1$, $u_1=0$, $u_2=-1$, with an expected total of **three collisions**. Trace that short case by hand before increasing the mass ratio. The aim is to explain why the event sequence and update rules are correct, not merely obtain a plausible final integer.
