### Concepts to be able to explain

- **Natural response versus driven response:** a circuit responding to previously stored energy is a different problem from a circuit continuously driven by an AC source. Decide which situation applies before choosing a method.
- **Initial conditions:** in the ideal switching models used here, capacitor voltage and inductor current are continuous. Establish their pre-switch values, then analyse the post-switch circuit separately.
- **Resonance versus selectivity:** the resonant frequency locates the response peak; bandwidth describes its width. Increasing series resistance reduces the height and sharpness of a current resonance.
- **Peak versus RMS:** a sinusoid's amplitude is not its RMS value. Use one consistent convention when calculating current, voltage and average power.
- **Energy versus power:** joules measure stored or transferred energy; watts measure its rate. A cycle's dissipated energy is average power multiplied by the period.

### A separate worked interpretation example

Suppose a measured resonance has a current amplitude of **100 mA** at its maximum. The half-power level is not 50 mA:

$$I_{\mathrm{half\ power}}=\frac{100}{\sqrt{2}}\approx70.7\ \mathrm{mA}.$$

The reason is that resistor power is proportional to current squared. At 50 mA the power would be one quarter of the maximum, not one half. This is an illustrative response-plot example, not a solution to today's worksheet.

**Takeaway:** identify the circuit, the quantity and the amplitude convention before substituting numbers.

**Useful preparation:** mark the step where your reasoning became uncertain: interpreting a switch, selecting an initial condition, converting an amplitude, or interpreting a resonance graph. Ask about that step rather than only comparing final numbers. The completed PS8 worked document remains separate from this brief.

## 10:00–13:00 · C8: timers, counters and PWM

**Confirmed laboratory: C8, Timers, Counters and PWM.** The first ELEC rotation row assigns today's morning slot. The session is led by the laboratory teaching team; an individual demonstrator is not named in the available allocation.

The practical builds a small sound synthesiser. A hardware timer produces an accurately timed tone; another PWM signal controls its amplitude. The important separation is **pitch versus loudness**: changing a waveform's repetition rate is not the same as changing its duty cycle or its amplitude.

### What the hardware is doing

A timer advances on clock ticks independently of the main program. A prescaler changes the tick rate. The counter mode determines whether it counts upwards, wraps, resets on a comparison, or counts up and down. An output-compare event can change a pin without a software delay loop.

Keep three different quantities distinct:

1. **Timer tick frequency:** how often the counter changes.
2. **Compare or overflow event frequency:** how often a chosen timer event occurs.
3. **Output waveform frequency:** how often the pin completes a whole high-and-low cycle.

A pin that toggles at each event needs two events to complete one waveform cycle. Do not automatically identify a timer event rate with the signal frequency.

### A short timing illustration

For a hypothetical output toggling every **0.5 ms**, one complete high-and-low cycle takes **1 ms**:

$$T=2(0.5\ \mathrm{ms})=1\ \mathrm{ms},\qquad f=\frac{1}{T}=1\ \mathrm{kHz}.$$

This is a counting illustration, not the C8 register configuration. The correct configuration and full preparation answers are in the separate lab-preparation document.

### PWM and amplitude

Duty cycle is the fraction of a period for which a signal is high. Changing duty cycle can change the average of a fast pulse stream without changing its repetition frequency. In the C8 synthesiser, the tone oscillator and the amplitude-control path perform different jobs. An oscilloscope trace should therefore be interpreted in terms of both time scale and signal path, not merely whether it “looks like a square wave”.

**Takeaway:** hardware timing frees the CPU, but the waveform still depends on the selected mode, output action and pin configuration.

**Useful preparation:** use the supplied **square.c**, **et_scale.h** and **melody.c**, rather than starting from an unrelated timer example. For each measured output, record the expected frequency, measured period and output pin. If the measured frequency differs by a factor of two, first count how many compare events form a complete cycle. Full solved preparation, register drawings and code stay outside this brief.
