## 09:00–10:00 · Circuits tutorial — phasors and impedance

**Lecturer: Professor Neil White. Confirmed dated allocation: Problem Sheet 5.** Today's tutorial concerns the sheet's phasor and impedance problems, rather than a new AC Power lecture. The published schedule contains a “9PM” typo; the personal timetable places this tutorial at **09:00**, consistent with the sheet's 09:00 deadline.

### What the sheet asks you to do

1. Convert complex numbers from rectangular form, such as $2-5j$, into a magnitude and an angle, then divide two phasors.
2. Distinguish a **phasor** from a time-varying signal. A phasor stores a sinusoid's amplitude and phase at a fixed frequency; the common time factor is omitted. Thus $1+8j$ can be a phasor, whereas $\sin(\omega t+\pi/4)$ is the actual waveform.
3. Use an impedance voltage divider to find the voltage across an inductor in a series RL circuit.
4. Find a load's complex impedance from its voltage and current, and identify a circuit that could have that impedance.

A resistor has impedance $R$; an inductor has $j\omega L$; a capacitor has $1/(j\omega C)$. Here $j^2=-1$, and **$\omega=2\pi f$** converts frequency in hertz into angular frequency in radians per second. Add series impedances in rectangular form; multiply or divide most easily in magnitude–angle form.

### Worked method: converting and dividing phasors

For the sheet's $V_1=2-5j$ and $V_2=1+6j$:

1. Calculate the first magnitude: $|V_1|=\sqrt{2^2+(-5)^2}=\sqrt{29}=5.385$.
2. Its real part is positive and imaginary part negative, so it is in the fourth quadrant: $\theta_1=\tan^{-1}(-5/2)=-68.20^\circ$.
3. Similarly, $|V_2|=\sqrt{1^2+6^2}=\sqrt{37}=6.083$, and $\theta_2=\tan^{-1}(6/1)=80.54^\circ$.
4. Divide the magnitudes and subtract the angles:
   $$\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{5.385}{6.083}\angle(-68.20^\circ-80.54^\circ)=\boxed{0.8853\angle-148.74^\circ}.$$
5. A rectangular check gives $(2-5j)(1-6j)/37=(-28-17j)/37=-0.7568-0.4595j$. Both components are negative, matching the third-quadrant angle.

**The common trap:** an ordinary inverse tangent does not identify the quadrant on its own. Use the signs of both components, or `atan2(imaginary, real)`.

### Worked method: an RL voltage divider

**Illustrative values, not the worksheet's numerical data:** take $R=200\,\Omega$, $L=50\,\mathrm{mH}$, $\omega=4000\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, and a 2 V peak input. These chosen values demonstrate the method; they are not an estimate of any unknown lab inductor.

1. Convert the inductance: $50\,\mathrm{mH}=0.050\,\mathrm H$.
2. Calculate $Z_L=j\omega L=j(4000)(0.050)=j200\,\Omega$.
3. The total series impedance is $200+j200=282.84\angle45^\circ\,\Omega$.
4. For output across the inductor,
   $$H=\frac{V_L}{V_{\rm in}}=\frac{j200}{200+j200}=0.7071\angle45^\circ.$$
5. Multiply the input amplitude by 0.7071: the output is **1.414 V peak**, leading the input by **45°**. With a sine-reference input $2\sin(4000t)$, write the output as $1.414\sin(4000t+\pi/4)$ V (with the phase expressed in radians).

The actual PS5 Question 3 supplies **100 Ω and 20 mH**, with $v_i(t)=5\sin(10^5t)$ V. Those are given circuit values, not values to infer experimentally.

**Takeaway:** keep the physical signal, its phasor and the circuit impedance distinct. **Useful prep:** make the phase reference explicit before converting the final complex answer back into a sine wave; the output-over-input phase is the quantity that determines lead or lag.

## 10:00–13:00 · T11 — RC Filters and Frequency Response

**Confirmed allocated lab: T11. This is a short briefing; the complete preparation remains separate.

### The central idea

The output is taken across the capacitor. At low frequency the capacitor's impedance is large, so most of the input appears at the output. At high frequency its impedance is smaller, so the output amplitude falls. This is a **low-pass filter**: it preserves slow changes more than fast ones.

These descriptions concern the same circuit:

- **Time response:** $\tau=RC$ controls how quickly the capacitor voltage changes after a step. A rising response completes about **63.2% of its total change after one time constant**. The curve is not a straight-line ramp.
- **Frequency response:** $H(j\omega)=1/(1+j\omega RC)$ describes the output/input amplitude ratio and phase. A sine-wave output has the same frequency as the input, but a smaller amplitude and a lag.
- **Simulation:** the handout uses MATLAB, including `ode23`, to compare numerical differential-equation solutions with the circuit's analytical behaviour. A numerical solver follows the rate of change; it does not make the circuit parameters measured facts.

The handout's Figure 1 model uses **1.59 kΩ and 0.1 µF**. Its separate preferred-value exercise leads to **1.5 kΩ and 0.1 µF**. These are distinct nominal models; neither is a measured component value.

### A useful interpretation example

**Illustrative amplitude ratio:** if a filter changes a 2.0 V peak sine wave into a 1.0 V peak sine wave:

1. The voltage gain is $1.0/2.0=0.5$.
2. Convert the voltage ratio to decibels: $20\log_{10}(0.5)=20(-0.3010)=\mathbf{-6.02\ dB}$.
3. The minus sign means attenuation. **Half the voltage is not −3 dB**; a voltage ratio of $1/\sqrt2\approx0.707$ is approximately −3 dB.

This connects directly to Neil White's **26 October lecture**, whose decibel section distinguishes $10\log_{10}$ for power ratios from $20\log_{10}$ for voltage ratios. It is **week-wide supporting context**, not a recording of today's lab. Interpreting a voltage ratio as a power ratio requires equal resistances.

**Takeaway:** the rounded step response and the reduced high-frequency sine-wave amplitude are two consequences of the same $RC$ time constant. **Useful prep:** keep the Figure 1 model and preferred-component model clearly labelled so their predictions are not accidentally mixed.
