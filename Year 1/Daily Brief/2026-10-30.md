## 09:00–10:00 · Circuits — power in capacitors and inductors

**Lecturer: Professor Neil White. Confirmed dated session: NMW09.** The Week 6 topic is AC Power. At **21:42–21:50 in Wednesday's recording**, Neil explicitly says the next session will finish power in capacitors and inductors. Maximum-power transfer follows in the same deck, but today's exact endpoint is not specified. The timetable's “9PM” entry is a typo; the class is at **09:00**.

### What to understand

- **A resistor dissipates energy:** voltage and current are in phase, so average power is positive. With RMS values, $P=I_{\rm rms}^2R$.
- **An ideal capacitor stores and returns energy:** current leads voltage by 90°. Its instantaneous power alternates between positive and negative values; its average real power over a cycle is zero.
- **An ideal inductor also stores and returns energy:** voltage leads current by 90°. It also has zero average real power in sinusoidal steady state. Real coils still have resistive losses.
- **Complex power keeps these effects separate:** $S=P+jQ$. $P$ is real power in watts, and $Q$ is reactive power in var. Under the passive sign convention, inductive loads have positive $Q$ and capacitive loads negative $Q$.
- **Use a consistent amplitude convention:** $S=VI^*$ for RMS phasors, or $S=\tfrac12VI^*$ for peak phasors. The star conjugates the current: reverse its phase angle before multiplying.

### Worked example: a purely reactive load

**Illustrative RMS values:** a capacitor has $V=10\angle0^\circ$ V and $I=2\angle90^\circ$ A.

1. Conjugate the current: $I^*=2\angle-90^\circ$ A.
2. Multiply: $S=10\times2\angle-90^\circ=-j20$ VA.
3. Read off the parts: **$P=0$ W and $Q=-20$ var**. Zero average power does not mean zero current or no energy exchange.
4. For an inductor with the same magnitudes, current would lag by 90°, giving **$S=+j20$ VA**, hence $Q=+20$ var.

**Further topic in the deck:** maximum average load power occurs with conjugate matching, $Z_L=Z_s^*$. For $Z_s=8+j6\,\Omega$, the matched load is $8-j6\,\Omega$: match the resistance and cancel the reactance. This is week-wide preparation, not a confirmed stopping point for today.

**Takeaway:** nonzero AC current can transfer energy back and forth without net energy consumption by an ideal component. **Useful prep:** label peak/RMS and phase lead/lag before calculating complex power.

## 12:00–13:00 · Digital Systems — ASM fundamentals

**Lecturer: Professor Geoff Merrett. Confirmed dated topic: ASM Design Part 1.** An **algorithmic state machine (ASM)** describes a synchronous circuit's states, transitions and outputs. The combined deck contains later examples too; today's first session is the fundamentals block.

### From a description to a circuit

1. Draw an **ASM chart** from the required behaviour. A rectangular box denotes a stored state, not a software instruction.
2. Give each state a binary code. Four states need two state bits, implemented with two D-type flip-flops.
3. Write a **state-transition table**: for every present state, state what the next state should be.
4. Derive Boolean expressions for the next-state bits. A D input must equal the value wanted at its corresponding Q output after the next active clock edge.
5. Implement those expressions with combinational logic feeding the state register. The state register supplies the memory; the logic calculates what comes next.

### Worked example: a two-bit counter

For $00\rightarrow01\rightarrow10\rightarrow11\rightarrow00$:

| Present $Q_1Q_0$ | Next $D_1D_0$ |
| --- | --- |
| 00 | 01 |
| 01 | 10 |
| 10 | 11 |
| 11 | 00 |

- The low bit always toggles, so **$D_0=\overline{Q_0}$**.
- The high bit toggles when $Q_0=1$, so **$D_1=Q_1\oplus Q_0$**.
- Both flip-flops share the clock and update together. The chart's arrows describe clocked transitions, not arbitrary delays through a software loop.

This derives the counter specified in the deck; the detailed board working for today's lecture is not yet available.

**Takeaway:** the transition table is the bridge between a behaviour specification and gate-level hardware. **Useful prep:** distinguish the present-state outputs $Q$ from the next-state inputs $D$ in every row.

Sources: [Dated Digital Systems schedule](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/course/view.php?id=901#section-1) · [ASM combined slides, Parts 1–2](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=36344).

## 15:00–16:00 · Digital Systems — inputs and outputs

**Lecturer: Professor Geoff Merrett. Confirmed dated topic: ASM Design Part 2: Outputs.** The matching deck's Part 2 includes conditional branches and inputs as well as outputs; later parity-checker design examples should not be assumed to be completed today.

- An **unconditional output**, shown inside a state box, depends on the current state only. “Unconditional” does not mean it is always on in every state.
- A **decision box** selects a path according to an input, allowing the same current state to have different next states.
- A **conditional output** depends on both the current state and an input condition.
- A **Moore machine** has outputs determined only by state. A **Mealy machine** has at least one output that also depends on inputs. An input-dependent output can change between clock edges, even though the state register remains clocked.
- The deck's divide-by-three up/down example uses states 00, 01 and 10, with MODE=0 for up and MODE=1 for down. The unused state 11 needs explicit consideration when implementing a robust circuit.

### Worked interpretation of the up/down example

At present state **01**:

1. With MODE=0, counting up gives next state **10**.
2. With MODE=1, counting down gives next state **00**.
3. The current state is identical in both cases. The different next state comes from the input, so the transition table needs columns for **both state and MODE**.
4. If an output is asserted simply because the circuit is in state 01, it is unconditional. If it is asserted only while in 01 **and** MODE=1, it is conditional.

**Takeaway:** keep three questions separate: what state is stored now, what state comes next, and what output is active now. **Useful prep:** trace one input change with the clock stopped, then one clock edge; this exposes the difference between state-dependent and input-dependent outputs.

Sources: [Dated module schedule](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/course/view.php?id=901#section-1) · [ASM slides: conditional branches, outputs and Moore/Mealy machines](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=36344).

## 16:00–17:00 · Mathematics — integration worked questions

**46/3001. Lecturer: Huajie Yi.** The Week 6 L1 recording ends at **40:01** with a plan to cover additional questions on Friday. Today's session is therefore a worked-question continuation of Integration 1; the exact question selection is not published.

- An **antiderivative** reverses differentiation. Include $+C$ until a supplied condition fixes it.
- A **definite integral** adds signed contributions. The fundamental theorem gives $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)$; reversing the bounds reverses the sign.
- **Substitution** reverses the chain rule. Change the differential as well as the expression, and change the bounds if keeping a definite integral in the new variable.
- **Integration by parts** reverses the product rule: $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$. Choose the differentiated factor so that the remaining integral becomes simpler.
- Useful reference primitives include $\int dx/(1+x^2)=\arctan x+C$ and $\int dx/\sqrt{1-x^2}=\arcsin x+C$, with the latter used on $-1<x<1$ for real-valued integration.

### Worked example from the preceding lecture

Find $\int t e^{2t}\,dt$:

1. Set $u=t$, so $du=dt$.
2. Set $dv=e^{2t}dt$, so $v=\tfrac12e^{2t}$.
3. Substitute into the parts formula:
   $$\int t e^{2t}dt=\frac12te^{2t}-\frac12\int e^{2t}dt.$$
4. Integrate the remaining exponential: $\int e^{2t}dt=\tfrac12e^{2t}$.
5. Therefore
   $$\boxed{\int t e^{2t}dt=\frac12te^{2t}-\frac14e^{2t}+C}.$$

The two factors of $\tfrac12$ come from integrating an exponential with inner coefficient 2. Differentiating the answer cancels the extra exponential terms and leaves $te^{2t}$.

**Takeaway:** choose the method from the structure of the integrand. **Useful prep:** if integration by parts makes the remaining integral harder, reconsider which factor you differentiated.

## 17:00–18:00 · Mathematics tutorial — Integration 1

**02A/2065. Tutorial team; individual tutor not specified.** The current Week 6 worksheet connects numerical approximation with exact antiderivatives. Its solutions are scheduled to become available **today at 16:00**.

### What the worksheet develops

1. Approximate $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2x\sin x\,dx$ using a Riemann sum with **eight intervals**, then evaluate the same integral by substitution.
2. For those eight intervals, $\Delta x=(2\pi-0)/8=\pi/4$. State the sample-point convention used for the rectangle heights.
3. Recognise an inner function and its derivative in $\int3x^2(x^3+1)\,dx$.
4. Integrate $\sin^2x$ and $\sin^2(-x)$. Since $\sin(-x)=-\sin x$, squaring makes the two integrands equal; a double-angle identity handles the square.
5. Find an antiderivative of $\sin x\sqrt{\cos x}$ subject to $F(\pi/2)=1$. The supplied value determines the integration constant, not another derivative.

### Short worked substitution

For $\int3x^2(x^3+1)\,dx$:

1. Let $u=x^3+1$.
2. Then $du=3x^2\,dx$, exactly the remaining factor.
3. The integral becomes $\int u\,du=\tfrac12u^2+C$.
4. Replace $u$: **$\tfrac12(x^3+1)^2+C$**.

**Takeaway:** numerical sums and exact integration describe the same signed accumulation; agreement is a useful check. **Useful prep:** keep the differential and changed bounds beside the substitution, rather than trying to reconstruct them at the end.
