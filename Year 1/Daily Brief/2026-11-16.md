### Concepts to follow

A capacitor stores electric-field energy, $E_C=\tfrac12 Cv^2$; an inductor stores magnetic-field energy, $E_L=\tfrac12 Li^2$. Resistance dissipates energy. At resonance, energy cycles between the reactive elements, while the source replaces the energy lost in the resistor.

The **quality factor** compares stored energy with energy lost in one cycle:

$$Q=2\pi\frac{\text{stored energy at resonance}}{\text{energy dissipated per cycle}}.$$

For a **series RLC circuit**,

$$\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad Q=\frac{\omega_0L}{R},\qquad \Delta\omega=\frac RL=\frac{\omega_0}{Q}.$$

Bandwidth is the separation between the two half-power frequencies. Half power corresponds to current amplitude falling to $1/\sqrt2$ of its resonant maximum, not half the current. Higher $Q$ means a sharper, narrower resonance; do not apply the series formula unchanged to a parallel topology.

**Worked example.** With $L=10\,\mathrm{mH}$, $C=1\,\mu\mathrm F$ and $R=10\,\Omega$, $\omega_0=10{,}000\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, so $f_0\approx1.59\,\mathrm{kHz}$. Then $Q=10$ and $\Delta f=\Delta\omega/(2\pi)\approx159\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Doubling the resistance halves $Q$ and doubles the bandwidth, while the ideal series resonant frequency stays the same.

**Takeaway:** resonance is not just a frequency; its sharpness tells you how much energy the circuit retains compared with how much it loses.

**Useful prep:** sketch the series RLC current response, label the resonant peak and half-power points, and distinguish Hz from radians per second. Recall Friday's caveat: an ideal lossless LC circuit driven exactly at resonance has no bounded sinusoidal steady state, so ordinary steady-state phasor power analysis is not valid there.

### Useful consolidation: serial communication

- **UART** is asynchronous: the receiver and transmitter agree on the bit rate and frame format rather than sharing a clock. Start and stop bits provide framing.
- **SPI** uses a clock and separate data directions, with a selection signal identifying the peripheral. Clock polarity and phase must agree.
- **I²C** shares clock and data lines between addressed devices. Open-drain signalling and pull-ups allow devices to share the bus without actively driving opposite logic levels.

**Worked example.** An 8N1 UART frame has one start bit, eight data bits and one stop bit: ten transmitted bits per byte. At 9,600 bit/s, the ideal continuous payload limit is $960$ bytes/s, before software delays or protocol overhead. Sending 100 bytes takes at least $100\times10/9600\approx104\,\mathrm{ms}$.

**Takeaway:** the advertised bit rate is not the same as useful byte throughput, and the electrical interface, framing and software configuration must all match.

**Useful prep:** be able to explain why SPI needs a clock but UART can operate without one, and trace a byte from a C variable through the peripheral's transmit register. Review the Il Matto pin assignments before assuming that a familiar desktop serial interface maps directly onto the microcontroller.

### Concepts to carry forward

A **controller** is a state machine that decides what happens next; the **datapath** contains the registers, counters and arithmetic hardware that do the work. Control signals such as `enable` or `load` go to the datapath; status signals such as `ready` return to the controller. This is usually more useful than trying to describe an entire processor as one enormous state machine.

A sequential testbench must provide a clock, exercise reset and apply meaningful inputs at sensible times. The 10 November lecture emphasises that a testbench itself needs debugging: inputs applied while reset is active may never test the intended behaviour, and changing an input exactly on the sampling edge can create a simulation race.

**Worked example.** Initialise a test clock low and toggle it every $5\,\mathrm{ns}$. Its period is $10\,\mathrm{ns}$, so its frequency is $100\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Rising edges occur at 5, 15 and 25 ns. For a positive-edge-triggered register, changing data at a falling edge gives it half a cycle to settle before the next rising edge. Start an active-low reset high, assert it low, then release it before the meaningful input sequence: that checks the reset transition rather than merely assuming the initial state is correct.

**Takeaway:** verification is about deliberately testing behaviour over time, not just producing a waveform that looks busy.

**Useful prep:** draw the first three clock cycles of a small testbench, label reset assertion/release and decide which edge samples the data. Be able to explain which signals belong to the controller and which belong to the datapath. The current module notes exclude the universal shift register and state-machine Version 2 from this year's syllabus; use current slides rather than relying on the older 2020 videos alone.
