## 09:00 · Circuits: power in resonant circuits

**Lecturer: Yoshi Tsuchiya.** Wednesday's lecture finished the RLC resonance example and introduced power; its closing preview explicitly deferred the next power slide to Friday. Today's useful continuation is therefore **power in resonance**, rather than repeating the entire resonance introduction. The session label in the dated timetable and the slide-deck number are different numbering systems.

### Concepts to bring together

A series RLC circuit has

$$Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right).$$

At $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ the reactive terms cancel, so $Z=R$ and source voltage and current are in phase. That does **not** mean the individual inductor and capacitor voltages, or their energy exchange, disappear.

Distinguish instantaneous power $p(t)=v(t)i(t)$, average real power $P$, and complex power $S=P+jQ$. **The lecture slides use peak-amplitude phasors**, giving

$$S=\tfrac12 VI^*.$$

With **RMS phasors**, the equivalent rule is $S=VI^*$. The conjugate matters, as does the factor of two. Under the passive sign convention a resistor absorbs positive real power; an ideal inductor has positive reactive power and an ideal capacitor negative reactive power. Their average real powers are zero.

### Worked preparation example

Use RMS quantities throughout: a series circuit at resonance has $V=10\text{ V}$, $R=20\,\Omega$, and $X_L=X_C=100\,\Omega$.

$$I=10/20=0.5\text{ A},\qquad P_R=I^2R=5\text{ W}.$$

$$S_L=jI^2X_L=j25\text{ var},\qquad S_C=-jI^2X_C=-j25\text{ var}.$$

Thus total load power is $5+j0\text{ VA}$: the source supplies 5 W, while the reactive powers cancel. If source power is instead expressed as **absorbed** power, its sign is negative. Each reactive component still has 50 V RMS across it.

**Takeaway:** resonance cancels net reactance, not every internal voltage or reactive power. Keep the peak/RMS convention and source/load sign convention explicit.

**Useful prep:** derive the three component powers from $VI^*$; explain why an ideal lossless LC circuit driven exactly at resonance cannot have the bounded sinusoidal steady state assumed by ordinary phasor analysis.

## 10:00 · Programming: serial communications preparation

**Current topic lecturer: Steve Gunn. Coverage: week-wide, not a confirmed Friday-specific topic.** The published slides and recording are Monday 9 November's Lecture 9; a distinct Friday topic is not available. The old “No lecture tomorrow” notice concerned 30 October, not today.

The central distinction is how a device knows **which bits belong together and when to sample them**:

- **UART:** asynchronous communication. Agree baud rate and framing at both ends; start and stop bits delimit a byte.
- **SPI:** synchronous communication using SCLK, MOSI, MISO and device select. Clock polarity/phase must match the peripheral; shifting transfers bits in both directions.
- **I²C:** shared SDA/SCL lines with pull-ups, device addresses, acknowledgements and start/stop conditions. I²C remains examinable even though its lab exercise is optional.
- **Firmware:** test the peripheral's status flags rather than assuming a write completes the physical transfer immediately.

### Worked preparation example

UART **8N1** sends one start bit, eight data bits, no parity bit and one stop bit: ten transmitted bits per payload byte. At 9,600 bit/s,

$$t_{\rm byte}=\frac{10}{9600}=1.042\text{ ms},\qquad
\text{ideal payload rate}=960\text{ bytes/s}.$$

Real pauses between frames reduce throughput further. “9,600 baud” is not “9,600 bytes per second”.

**Takeaway:** distinguish data from framing overhead, and explain why UART needs agreed timing while SPI/I²C distribute a clock.

**Useful prep:** sketch the signal lines for all three interfaces; explain chip select versus addressing; trace a transmit routine that waits for the relevant status flag. This is interface preparation, not a solved lab exercise.

**Sources:** [course](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/course/view.php?id=899); [Lecture 9 slides](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=36218); [9 November recording](https://southampton.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=d28b2bd8-62b7-458c-b68e-b4fd0107ed0f), especially 11:54–12:07 and 37:18 onward; [announcements](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/forum/view.php?id=36210).

## 12:00 · Digital Systems: from a specification to an ASM design

**Lecturer: Professor Geoff Merrett.** The draft schedule labels noon “ASM Design Part 5: Design Example 2b”, but the 6 November recording already completed pedestrian-crossing Part 2. Geoff's closing preview says the next week's first lecture starts **past-paper ASM design examples**. That preview is the better guide to progression; the exact examples and their division between today's two lectures remain unconfirmed.

An algorithmic state machine must represent what the system remembers, not merely list input names. Separate **states**, **input decisions**, **next-state changes** and **outputs**. Moore outputs depend only on state; Mealy outputs may also depend on current inputs.

### Worked recap: pedestrian crossing

In the handwritten example, $Q=0$ means traffic-go and $Q=1$ pedestrian-go. Let $B$ be the button and $T$ indicate that the timer has finished:

$$Q^+=B\overline Q+\overline TQ,\qquad RESET\_TIMER=B\overline Q.$$

$$TG=PR=\overline Q,\qquad TR=PG=Q.$$

With $Q=0,B=1$, reset the timer and enter pedestrian-go at the next clock edge. With $Q=1,T=0$, remain there. When $T=1$, return to traffic-go. A timer reset asserted throughout the waiting state would continually restart it, so the intended wait could never finish.

**Takeaway:** words such as “when pressed”, “while waiting” and “until finished” determine whether an output is a transition action or a sustained state output.

**Useful prep:** reconstruct the state/input table from these equations, then recover the ASM chart. Check button-held-high behaviour rather than assuming all inputs are single-cycle pulses.

**Sources:** [dated draft schedule and course](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/course/view.php?id=901); [ASM slides](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=36344); [handwritten Part 2, page 5](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=45975); [6 November Part 4 recording](https://southampton.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=72cae18a-a29f-48a8-a655-b4fa00f73b6b), 21:22–30:16 and next-week preview at 34:14.

## 15:00 · Digital Systems: exam-style controller design

**Lecturer: Professor Geoff Merrett. Coverage: continuation; exact exercise unconfirmed.** The preview names a lift controller, keyless car entry and a divide-by-three counter with pause, with further examples if time permits. These are preparation targets, not a claim that every one will be taught today.

The important step is translating a written specification into a **small, complete state model**. Choose state encodings, derive outputs, then check reset, boundary cases and excluded input combinations. Only simplify the Boolean logic after the state behaviour is right.

### Illustrative example from the published slides

A lift controller remembers occupancy 0, 1, 2 or 3, encoded $00,01,10,11$. The permitted maximum is two; the lights are on whenever occupied and the alarm sounds when over capacity:

$$LIGHTS=Q_1\lor Q_0,\qquad ALARM=Q_1Q_0.$$

An ENTER event moves occupancy $1\to2$; EXIT moves $2\to1$; reaching three asserts the alarm. The exercise excludes simultaneous entry/exit and assumes nobody enters while the alarm sounds. Those assumptions constrain the design; do not silently invent different behaviour.

**Takeaway:** states encode remembered information. Test the awkward transitions, not just a typical sequence.

**Useful prep:** try a first ASM chart before looking at its worked solution, then annotate which outputs depend only on state and which depend on inputs. Problem Sheet 4 is scheduled to **release today at 15:00**; this is a release time, not a verified hand-in deadline.

## 16:00 · Mathematics: first-order differential equations

**Lecturer for the ODE block: Bing Chu. Coverage: confirmed Week 8 topic; Friday's exact subsection is not specified.** This week's preparation is first-order ODEs. Second-order linear ODEs belong to next week's preparation, not automatically to today's lecture.

An ODE relates an unknown function to its derivatives. A general solution contains an arbitrary constant; an initial condition selects a particular solution. Always verify both the differential equation and the initial condition.

### Method map

- **Direct integration:** $y'=f(x)$ gives $y=\int f(x)\,dx+C$.
- **Separation:** rewrite $y'=f(x)g(y)$ as $dy/g(y)=f(x)\,dx$. Before dividing by $g(y)$, check whether its zeros give constant solutions.
- **Exact equations:** for $M\,dx+N\,dy=0$, test $M_y=N_x$ and seek a potential $F$ with $F_x=M,F_y=N$.
- **Linear equations:** put the equation into $y'+p(x)y=r(x)$ first. The integrating factor is $\mu=e^{\int p(x)dx}$, giving $(\mu y)'=\mu r$.
- **Bernoulli equations:** the week's theory also includes $y'+py=ry^a$, reduced to a linear equation by $u=y^{1-a}$ when $a\ne1$.

### Worked preparation example

Solve $y'+2y=6$, $y(0)=1$. The integrating factor is $e^{2x}$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}(e^{2x}y)=6e^{2x}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=3+Ce^{-2x}.$$

The initial condition gives $C=-2$, so $y=3-2e^{-2x}$. Substitution checks it: $y'=4e^{-2x}$ and $y'+2y=6$. The transient decays towards the equilibrium value 3, just as a first-order circuit approaches its steady state.

**Takeaway:** recognising the form saves more effort than trying an integration technique at random. Integration skills are now tools for solving a model, not just isolated exercises.

**Useful prep:** watch the four first-order ODE videos and use Kreyszig, *Advanced Engineering Mathematics*, 10th edition, Chapter 1. Practise recognising separation versus a linear integrating factor before doing long calculations.

**Sources:** [6 November ODE announcement](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=2707); [Week 8/9 preparation](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/page/view.php?id=44582); [first-order theory slides](https://moodle.ecs.soton.ac.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=42855); [four-video playlist](https://southampton.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?pid=df05aa69-c12a-4b4b-a1a4-b4f900aae582). The introductory video links physical modelling, solution and interpretation, then distinguishes general solutions from initial-value problems.

## 17:00 · Mathematics tutorial: choosing and checking a method

**Tutor name: not specified in the available materials.** The current first-order ODE sheet has five questions: direct integration, separation with an initial condition, two exact-equation exercises, and a linear integrating-factor problem. Bring attempted working and mark the step where the method becomes unclear.

### Worked model from Question 2

For $y'=-4x/y$ with $y(2)=3$,

$$y\,dy=-4x\,dx\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^2=-4x^2+C.$$

Substitution gives $C=25$. The positive initial value selects

$$y=\sqrt{25-4x^2},\qquad -\tfrac52<x<\tfrac52.$$

The interval matters: the original equation is undefined at $y=0$, even though the implicit curve can be drawn further as a geometric object.

For an exact-equation check, Question 3 has $M=2xy$, $N=x^2$, so $M_y=N_x=2x$. Its potential is $F=x^2y$, yielding $x^2y=C$. This demonstrates why integrating $M$ with respect to $x$ initially needs an unknown **function of $y$**, not just a numerical constant.

**Takeaway:** a final expression alone is not enough. State the branch/domain when relevant and check the original equation, especially after division or taking a square root.

**Useful prep:** for Question 5, identify $p(x)=\sin x$ and obtain $\mu=e^{-\cos x}$ before expanding anything. For Question 4, be alert to the initial point and whether an implicit solution is a differentiable graph there; this is a useful tutorial question rather than a reason to force an invalid branch.
