## 09:00 · Digital Systems: Simulation and Synthesis

**Mark Zwolinski.** The dated Digital schedule lists Simulation; Lecture 8 covers simulation races, synthesis constraints and state encoding. A race can arise when a testbench changes a signal at the same simulated instant a clocked design samples it. Synthesis, by contrast, maps the register and logic behaviour to hardware under constraints such as clock period and area.

**Example:** if a rising clock edge and a change in input $D$ both occur at 10 ns, a poorly arranged test can observe either the old or the new value. Drive $D$ before the sampling edge, then check $Q$ after the registered update. Moving stimulus removes that ambiguity; it does not repair a hardware timing violation.

**Takeaway and prep:** distinguish testbench scheduling from the behaviour of the circuit. Be ready to explain why a clocked assignment uses the old input values at the sampling edge and why a synthesis timing constraint is not an HDL delay statement. **Example:** an output register with write-enable low must hold its previous value even if its data input changes. With write-enable high it captures the input at the active edge. Use the same distinction when tracing a memory-mapped write: the address alone does not authorise a write.

**Takeaway and prep:** keep address selection, the write-enable condition and the clocked update separate. Bring a short expected trace for the first piece you plan to build, with one case that writes and one that must not. ## 17:00 · Mathematics: Linear Algebra

**Paolo Rapisarda.** This week's block covers vectors, subspaces, linear combinations, span, independence, bases and introductory linear transformations. The exact split between lectures is not specified. The lecturer assigned **sections 1.1–1.4** and suggested trying **self-study questions 1–5 on page 46** ahead of the lectures. The Friday 27 November afternoon tutorial is a drop-in for the first page of the tutorial sheet.

**Example:** $(1,2)$ and $(2,4)$ are dependent because the second is twice the first. All their linear combinations therefore stay on the line through $(1,2)$, not across the whole plane. Replacing $(2,4)$ by $(0,1)$ gives an independent pair; $(a,b)=a(1,2)+(b-2a)(0,1)$, so the new pair spans $\mathbb R^2$.

**Takeaway and prep:** span asks what combinations can reach; independence asks whether any vector is redundant. Try showing both properties on the same small vector set.
