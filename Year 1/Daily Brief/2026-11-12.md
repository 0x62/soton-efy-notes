### From component reactance to current

A sinusoidal source driving a series resistor, inductor and capacitor sees the impedance

$$Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right).$$

Impedance plays the role of resistance for sinusoidal signals, while also describing the phase difference between voltage and current. The real part is the resistance $R$. The imaginary part is the net reactance: inductive reactance $\omega L$ minus capacitive reactance $1/(\omega C)$. Frequency in hertz must first be converted to angular frequency with $\omega=2\pi f$.

The two quantities needed to calculate the current are

$$|Z|=\sqrt{R^2+\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2},\qquad
\phi=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\omega L-1/(\omega C)}{R}\right),\quad R>0.$$

With source voltage taken as the zero-phase reference, current magnitude is $V_{\mathrm{RMS}}/|Z|$ and its phase is $-\phi$.

- Below resonance, the net reactance is negative. Current leads the voltage.
- At resonance, the reactances cancel, leaving $Z=R$. Current and source voltage are in phase.
- Above resonance, the net reactance is positive. Current lags the voltage.

This also explains the graph: impedance magnitude reaches a minimum at resonance, while admittance magnitude $|Y|=1/|Z|$ and current magnitude reach a maximum. Resistance keeps that peak finite in a real series RLC circuit.

### Worked example: a point below resonance

These are illustrative values, not answers to an assessed sheet. Take $R=20\,\Omega$, $L=50\,\mathrm{mH}$, $C=20\,\mathrm{\mu F}$, a 10 V RMS source and $\omega=500\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$.

1. Convert the units: $L=0.050\,\mathrm H$ and $C=20\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm F$.
2. Find the inductor's reactance: $X_L=500\times0.050=25\,\Omega$.
3. Find the capacitor's reactance: $X_C=1/(500\times20\times10^{-6})=100\,\Omega$.
4. Combine them: $Z=20+j(25-100)=20-j75\,\Omega$.
5. Calculate the magnitude: $|Z|=\sqrt{20^2+75^2}=\sqrt{6025}\approx77.62\,\Omega$.
6. Calculate current: $I_{\mathrm{RMS}}=10/77.62\approx0.129\,\mathrm A$.
7. Calculate impedance angle: $\phi=\tan^{-1}(-75/20)\approx-75.1^\circ$. Current therefore leads the source by $75.1^\circ$.

For the same components, resonance occurs at

$$\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.050(20\times10^{-6})}}=1000\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},$$

so $f_0=1000/(2\pi)\approx159.2\,\mathrm{Hz}$. At that frequency, current rises to $10/20=0.50\,\mathrm A$ RMS. The current can be larger even though the source voltage has not changed, because the total impedance is smaller.

**Takeaway:** calculate the signed net reactance before interpreting the phase. A triangle below the horizontal axis is appropriate when capacitive reactance dominates. Keep RMS values together throughout a calculation; do not substitute a peak voltage into an RMS-current calculation.

### UART: observe the program while it runs

UART sends bits without a separate clock wire. The transmitter and receiver agree on the baud rate and framing, and the receiver uses the start bit to establish when to sample the data. Connect TX to RX, RX to TX and ground to ground.

In C7, the supplied debug interface connects C's standard input and output to the board's UART. After initialisation, a `printf` can expose the value of a program variable on the PC terminal. This is useful when the display alone does not reveal whether the program has calculated the right value.

For a 9600-baud, 8-N-1 frame, one byte requires one start bit, eight data bits and one stop bit:

$$t_{\mathrm{byte}}=\frac{1+8+1}{9600}=0.0010417\,\mathrm s\approx1.04\,\mathrm{ms}.$$

Thus the ideal continuous payload rate is 960 bytes per second, not 9600 bytes per second. Printing too much can slow a program that waits for each transmission.

### SPI: turn a number into a physical output

SPI adds an explicit clock. A controller shifts bits towards the peripheral on MOSI while reading bits on MISO; clock polarity and phase must match the peripheral's requirements. A command has meaning only when its bits are grouped and timed as the peripheral expects.

The C7 peripheral is the **MCP4261 dual digital potentiometer**. Changing a wiper code changes its output voltage. The later graphics work uses the two channels as X and Y outputs, connecting a software coordinate to a physical voltage. An illustrative ideal unloaded half-scale wiper code gives

$$V_w=3.3\times\frac{128}{256}=1.65\,\mathrm V.$$

This is a nominal calculation, not a measured value. Loading and device tolerances can change the actual result.

### I²C: address a device on shared wires

I²C uses a shared clock and data line. Each target has an address, and acknowledgement bits show whether a byte was accepted. The pull-up resistors bring the lines high; devices pull them low or release them. This differs electrically from simply driving a line high and low.

C7's **MCP7940M real-time clock** stores time in registers. Reading time therefore means sending an address and register selection, then receiving bytes and interpreting their bit fields. The optional clock/graphics extension combines this with the potentiometer outputs.

Monday's lecture clarified that **UART and SPI are the standard C7 work; the I²C lab extension is optional, but I²C remains examinable** (11:48–12:12). That distinction is relevant when interpreting the manual's broader list of outcomes.

**Specific hardware caution:** the manual warns that the C232HM cable's TX line can back-power the microcontroller. Switching off the board may therefore fail to produce the reset expected. This can explain why an old program appears to continue after a supposed power cycle.

**Takeaway:** distinguish the data value from the transfer that carries it. UART adds frame bits, SPI requires a clocked command sequence, and I²C includes addressing and acknowledgements. The logic analyser makes those transfers visible. The full preparation answers and supplied-function explanations are in the separate C7 preparation document, not repeated here.
