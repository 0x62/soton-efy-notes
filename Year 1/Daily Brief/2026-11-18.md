### What to understand

A **counter** stores a number and changes it in response to events. A **timer** uses regular clock events, turning a count into a duration. The timer peripheral can run independently while the CPU does useful work, unlike a busy-wait delay that occupies the processor.

For the ATmega644P, timers 0 and 2 have **8-bit** counters; timer 1 has a **16-bit** counter. These hold 0–255 and 0–65,535 respectively. The larger range allows longer intervals at the same tick rate, or finer timing resolution for a given interval.

Three separate choices matter:

1. **Clock and prescaler:** how often the count changes. With CPU clock $f_{\rm CPU}$ and divisor $N$, the timer tick frequency is $f_{\rm CPU}/N$.
2. **Counting mode and TOP:** whether the counter wraps at its maximum, resets on a comparison, or counts both up and down.
3. **Action:** whether an event sets a flag, changes an output pin, or requests an interrupt. Gunn places interrupts later in the module; this week's emphasis is hardware-generated output waveforms.

**Do not use one frequency formula for every mode.** Counting up and resetting, counting up and down, and toggling a pin produce different relationships between timer events and a complete waveform cycle.

### Worked timing illustration

Take the lecture's **12 MHz** CPU clock with a prescaler of **8**:

$$f_{\rm tick}=\frac{12\times10^6}{8}=1.5\text{ MHz},\qquad T_{\rm tick}\approx0.667\,\mu\text{s}.$$

An 8-bit timer in ordinary free-running mode completes 256 counts per overflow, so its overflow interval is

$$T_{\rm overflow}=256T_{\rm tick}\approx170.7\,\mu\text{s}.$$

That is an **overflow interval**, not automatically the period of an output square wave. First identify the selected mode and precisely what makes the output change. This example is a timing check, not a C8 configuration recipe.

**Takeaway:** count the clock ticks and output transitions explicitly before setting registers.

**Useful clinic preparation:** bring one question about prescaler/range, one timing sketch, and a minimal program if an output is missing. Separate a wrong timer frequency from a pin that has not been configured as an output. If your uncertainty is about PWM, distinguish the repetition frequency from the duty cycle, which is the fraction of each period spent high.

### From resonance to selectivity

For a sinusoidally driven **series RLC circuit**,

$$Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right),\qquad \omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.$$

At resonance the reactive terms cancel and the current is largest. The quality factor describes how sharply concentrated that response is around resonance. The deck connects it to energy stored versus energy dissipated per cycle, then to the width of the resonance curve.

For this series circuit,

$$Q=\frac{\omega_0L}{R},\qquad \Delta\omega=\omega_H-\omega_L=\frac{R}{L},\qquad Q=\frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega}.$$

The bandwidth boundaries are the **half-power frequencies**. Since resistor power is proportional to current squared, half the maximum power means current amplitude $1/\sqrt2$ of its peak, not half its peak. With fixed source voltage, the same ratio applies to the admittance magnitude.

- Higher $Q$ means a narrower response relative to the resonant frequency.
- Increasing series resistance lowers $Q$ and broadens the bandwidth.
- Use consistent units: $Q=f_0/\Delta f$ in hertz, or $Q=\omega_0/\Delta\omega$ in radians per second. Do not mix the two.
- The half-power frequencies are not generally symmetric about $\omega_0$ on a linear frequency axis. The deck obtains them from a quadratic; their product is $\omega_0^2$.

### Worked illustration

For $L=0.10\,\mathrm H$, $C=10\,\mu\mathrm F$ and $R=10\,\Omega$:

$$\omega_0=1000\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\quad f_0\approx159.2\,\mathrm{Hz},\quad Q=10.$$

The bandwidth is $\Delta\omega=100\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, or $\Delta f\approx15.9\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Solving $|\omega L-1/(\omega C)|=R$ gives approximately $\omega_L=951.2$ and $\omega_H=1051.2\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$. Their difference is 100, while their geometric mean is 1000.

**Takeaway:** a resonance frequency tells you where the peak is; Q and bandwidth tell you how selective the circuit is.

**Useful prep:** sketch the current/admittance peak, mark its $1/\sqrt2$ amplitude level and the two crossings, then explain how a larger resistance changes the picture. Connect this to the frequency sweeps in the recent RLC laboratory, without confusing a measured response with an ideal component model.

## 11:00–12:00 · Weekly tutorial: an RC low-pass filter

**Confirmed activity: ELEC1311 Activity 2**, assigned to ELEC in the week beginning 16 November. Neil White's linked **Tutorial Problem Sheet 2** shows a resistor in series with a capacitor, with the output measured **across the capacitor**. Your tutor leads the session; the timetable does not specify the tutor or room.

The sheet asks for the transfer function and its magnitude, the zero- and infinite-frequency limits, the frequency where the gain is $1/\sqrt2$, and a plot explaining the circuit's purpose.

Start with $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)$ and the impedance divider:

$$H(j\omega)=\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}=\frac{1}{1+j\omega RC},\qquad |H|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}}.$$

At low frequency the capacitor has a large impedance and the output approaches the input. At high frequency its impedance becomes small and the output approaches zero. This is a **low-pass filter**. The corner is $\omega_c=1/(RC)$, where the magnitude is $1/\sqrt2$ and the phase is $-45^\circ$.

### A scale for the sketch

Using illustrative values $R=1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and $C=100\,\mathrm{nF}$ gives $RC=100\,\mu\mathrm s$, hence $f_c\approx1.59\,\mathrm{kHz}$. At ten times the corner frequency the gain is $1/\sqrt{101}\approx0.0995$, about $-20\,\mathrm{dB}$.

**Takeaway:** the output location determines the filter. Moving the output to the resistor produces a different transfer function, even though the components are unchanged.

**Useful prep:** attempt the divider derivation yourself, label both frequency units on your sketch, and be ready to explain why $-3\,\mathrm{dB}$ corresponds to about 0.707 voltage gain. Today's RC example and the lecture's RLC bandwidth use the same half-power idea in different response shapes.
