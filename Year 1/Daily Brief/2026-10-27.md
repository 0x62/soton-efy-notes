## 09:00–10:00 · Digital Systems: Problem Sheet 1 feedback

**Lecturer: Jize Yan. Confirmed scheduled coverage: feedback on Problem Sheet 1.** This is not another Combinational SystemVerilog lecture. The worksheet spans combinational logic, truth tables, gates, decoders, multiplexers and Boolean simplification; which individual questions receive most attention is not specified.

### What the feedback is about

- **Combinational versus sequential logic:** a combinational output depends on the present inputs. A sequential system also needs stored state: a washing machine must remember whether it is washing, rinsing or spinning.
- **Truth tables:** with $n$ binary inputs there are $2^n$ input combinations. A three-input AND is 1 only for `111`, so seven rows produce 0. An $n$-input OR is 0 only for the all-zero row, so $2^n-1$ rows produce 1.
- **From a truth-table row to a circuit:** write an AND term containing every input, complementing the inputs that are 0. For `ABC = 101`, the term is $A\bar BC$. OR together the terms for the rows where the required output is 1.
- **Decoder versus multiplexer:** a decoder selects one output corresponding to its binary input; a multiplexer selects one data input. Both can implement a truth table, but the wiring strategy differs.
- **Simplification:** Boolean algebra removes redundant terms without changing the truth table. For example, $AB+ABC=AB(1+C)=AB$. This is absorption, not ordinary arithmetic addition.

### Worked worksheet example: a multiplexer implementing XOR

The sheet gives a four-to-one multiplexer with selectors `AB`: `00 → C1`, `01 → C2`, `10 → C3`, `11 → C4`. Set its data inputs to `0, 1, 1, 0`.

1. For `AB = 10`, input `C3` is selected, so the output is **1**.
2. For `AB = 11`, input `C4` is selected, so the output is **0**.
3. Across all four selector combinations the output sequence is `0, 1, 1, 0`: it is 1 exactly when the two selectors differ. Therefore the circuit implements **$A\oplus B$**.

**Takeaway:** translate between words, a truth table, a Boolean expression and a circuit without changing the function. **Useful prep:** compare your own Problem Sheet 1 answers at those translation steps; they are more informative than memorising gate names alone.

Two experiments have different aims:

- **Measure the unknown actual inductance.** Observe the transient response of an inductor and known resistor, then use the response time and resistance to determine $L$. The distinction between resistance in the ideal model and resistance actually present in the circuit matters when interpreting the result.
- **Observe a nonlinear oscillator.** The inductor–diode circuit can change from a waveform that repeats every cycle to one that repeats every two cycles, then four. This is **period doubling**. A **bifurcation** is the change in the pattern as an input parameter is varied; it does not mean that the circuit has become random.
- **Attach uncertainty to the result.** A value alone cannot support the manual's requested comparison with a stated or accepted value. The uncertainty describes the range supported by the measurements and the method.

**Short conceptual example:** if the input repeats every $T$ but two successive output cycles differ and the pair repeats, the output period is $2T$. That is the first period-doubling pattern—not merely a larger-amplitude sine wave.

### Core ideas

- **An antiderivative reverses differentiation.** If $F'(x)=f(x)$, then $\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C$. The constant is needed because differentiating any constant gives zero.
- **A definite integral is signed accumulation.** A Riemann sum approximates it by adding thin rectangles, each with height $f(x)$ and width $\Delta x$. Contributions below the horizontal axis are negative, so signed integral and total geometric area are not always the same.
- **The fundamental theorem connects the two views.** For continuous $f$ and an antiderivative $F$, $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)$. Conversely, the derivative of $\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is $f(x)$.
- **Substitution reverses the chain rule.** Choose an inner expression as a new variable, then replace its derivative together with $dx$. The Week 6 tutorial includes both Riemann sums and substitution, plus trigonometric identities.

### Worked slide example: finding the constant

Find the antiderivative of $\cos x$ whose value at $x=\pi/2$ is $3/2$.

1. Integrate: $F(x)=\sin x+C$.
2. Substitute the given point: $\sin(\pi/2)+C=3/2$.
3. Since $\sin(\pi/2)=1$, subtract 1: $C=1/2$.
4. Therefore **$F(x)=\sin x+1/2$**. Differentiating gives $\cos x$, and the specified point also checks.

### Worked Week 6 example: substitution

For $\int 3x^2(x^3+1)\,dx$:

1. Put $u=x^3+1$.
2. Differentiate: $du=3x^2\,dx$.
3. Replace both parts: $\int u\,du=u^2/2+C$.
4. Substitute back: **$(x^3+1)^2/2+C$**.
5. Check by differentiating: $\tfrac12\cdot2(x^3+1)\cdot3x^2=3x^2(x^3+1)$.

**Takeaway:** distinguish an indefinite family of functions from a definite numerical accumulation, and check an integration result by differentiating it. **Useful prep:** recognise the derivative of the inner expression before attempting substitution. The Week 6 guidance also notes that theorem numbering differs between the prerecorded videos and notes; use the theorem's statement rather than its number to match them.
